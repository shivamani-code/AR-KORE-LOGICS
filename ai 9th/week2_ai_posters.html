// THE 6 STAGES — IN ORDER
1 · Problem Scoping 2 · Data Acquisition 3 · Data Exploration 4 · Modelling 5 · Evaluation 6 · Deployment
🎯
1. Problem Scoping
Define WHAT problem you are solving, WHO is affected, WHERE it happens, and WHY it matters. The most critical stage — a poorly defined problem always leads to a useless AI solution. Use the 4Ws canvas!
🗃️
2. Data Acquisition
Collect the data your AI needs to learn from. Identify sources, collect raw data, ensure it is relevant and unbiased. No data = no AI. Bad data = dangerous AI that gives wrong answers!
📊
3. Data Exploration
Understand your data through statistics and graphs. Find patterns, spot missing values, and check for outliers. This stage tells you if your data is good enough to train an AI on.
🤖
4. Modelling
Choose and train an AI model — rule-based or learning-based. Feed it your prepared data. The model learns patterns and relationships. This is where the "magic" of AI happens!
✅
5. Evaluation
Test how well your AI model performs using True Positive, False Positive, True Negative, False Negative. Low accuracy? Go back and improve the data or change the model!
🚀
6. Deployment
Make the AI available for real users — as an app, website, or device. Monitor its performance. Update when it makes mistakes. AI is NEVER truly "finished" — it keeps improving!
// CASE STUDY: PREVENTABLE BLINDNESS — ALL 6 STAGES IN REAL LIFE
🎯 Stage 1 — Problem Scoping
Millions of diabetic patients in rural India go blind each year because the nearest eye doctor is 200 km away. Problem statement: "How can we screen for diabetic eye disease in villages without a specialist present?" Clear, specific, measurable.
🗃️ Stage 2 — Data Acquisition
Google partnered with Aravind Eye Hospital, Madurai. Collected 128,175 labelled retinal photographs — each marked "diabetic" or "healthy" by certified ophthalmologists. That is the training dataset!
📊 Stage 3 — Data Exploration
Researchers checked image quality, balanced the dataset, removed blurry images, and ensured diverse representation of ages, skin tones, and disease stages. Good data prep = better model performance.
🤖 Stage 4 — Modelling
Google trained a deep learning model (CNN). It learned to spot tiny blood vessel changes in the retina — changes even trained doctors sometimes miss on a bad day!
✅ Stage 5 — Evaluation
Tested on 12,000 images. Result: 94.5% sensitivity (finds disease when present). This MATCHED or EXCEEDED specialist ophthalmologist accuracy. Deployment could proceed!
🚀 Stage 6 — Deployment
Deployed in rural health centres in India and Thailand. A nurse takes a retinal photo. AI gives a result in 60 seconds. No specialist needed on-site. Hundreds of thousands of lives changed!
😂

// THE 4Ws CANVAS — YOUR THINKING TOOL FOR EVERY AI PROJECT
WHAT — THE PROBLEM
What exactly?
Be ultra-specific. Not "pollution is bad" — but "PM2.5 levels in Hyderabad exceed safe limits on 200+ days/year causing 3,000 respiratory hospital admissions annually." The more specific your WHAT, the more targeted your AI solution can be!
WHO — THE PEOPLE AFFECTED
Who exactly?
Name the exact population. Not "students" but "Class IX students in government schools in Secunderabad who lack home internet access." Age, location, economic group, and language all matter. The AI solution must serve THESE specific people.
WHERE — THE CONTEXT
Where exactly?
Which city, neighbourhood, school, or hospital? Does the problem happen indoors or outdoors? At what time of day? Context shapes what data you need — a traffic AI for Secunderabad needs Hyderabad road data, not Bengaluru data!
WHY — THE IMPORTANCE
Why does it matter?
What happens if this problem is NOT solved? How many people suffer? What is the cost — economic, human, environmental? Connect to a UN SDG here. "This connects to SDG 6 — Clean Water because..." makes your project globally relevant.
// EXAMPLE CANVAS — FULLY FILLED IN
🌊 Example: Water Wastage in School Toilets
WHAT
Taps in school toilet blocks are left running during lunch break, wasting approx. 500 litres per school per day. No existing sensor or timer system is installed.
WHO
500 students + 40 staff at KV Hyderabad. Long-term: community water supply in a city already facing 45-day summer water reserves.
WHERE
4 toilet blocks, 3 taps each. Wastage peaks 1–2 PM (lunch) and 8–9 AM (arrival). Inside school campus, Secunderabad.
WHY
1,200 Hyderabad schools × 500L/day = 600,000 litres DAILY wasted. Connects to SDG 6: Clean Water & Sanitation. AI smart-tap sensors could cut waste by 80%.
// WEAK vs STRONG PROBLEM STATEMENTS
❌ Weak Statement
"Traffic in Hyderabad is very bad and causes lots of problems for everyone every day."
Why weak? Too vague. What KIND of problem? Which junction? Who EXACTLY is affected? How is it MEASURED? An AI engineer cannot build anything from this!
✅ Strong Statement
"At Mehdipatnam Junction, peak-hour ambulance wait time exceeds 12 minutes, causing 40% of ambulances to arrive late to Niloufer Hospital between 5–8 PM on weekdays."
Specific junction ✅ Specific time ✅ Measurable outcome (12 min, 40%) ✅ Real impact (hospital arrivals) ✅ AI engineer can start building!

// WHAT IS A MIND MAP IN AN AI PROJECT?
🌿 Mind Map — Exploring the Full Problem Space
A Mind Map is a visual diagram that starts with a central topic and branches out into related sub-topics. In an AI project, it helps you explore ALL dimensions of your problem before narrowing to one specific focus. It prevents tunnel vision — making sure you don't miss a crucial part of the problem. Think of it as a full brain-dump on paper before committing to a direction. Real AI engineers draw mind maps before writing a single line of code!
// HOW TO DRAW AN AI PROJECT MIND MAP — 5 STEPS
  • 1

    Write the Central Topic in a Circle

    Put your broad problem theme in the centre. Example: "Air Quality in Hyderabad" or "School Dropout Rate." Keep it broad at this stage — you'll narrow down later. Use a big circle and bold text so it stands out.

  • 2

    Draw 5–6 Main Branches

    Thick lines radiating outward from the centre. Label each one: Causes, Effects, Who Is Affected, Current Solutions, Available Data, AI Possibilities. These are your thinking categories.

  • 3

    Add Sub-Branches with Specifics

    Thin lines from each main branch. "Causes" → vehicles, factories, construction dust, crop burning, diwali crackers, etc. Get as specific and creative as you can. No idea is too small!

  • 4

    Colour-Code and Add Icons

    Each main branch gets a different colour. Add small sketch icons. Research shows visual mind maps are remembered 65% better than plain text notes. Make it look alive!

  • 5

    Circle ONE Focus Area to Develop

    Look at your map. Which sub-problem (a) can AI genuinely help solve AND (b) has data you can realistically collect in your city? Circle it. This becomes your project's specific focus and feeds directly into your 4Ws Canvas!

// THE FORMULA FOR A PERFECT PROBLEM STATEMENT
📝 Fill-in-the-Blanks Formula
"[Specific group of people] experience [specific measurable problem] in [specific location/context], causing [specific negative outcome with a number]. Currently, [existing solutions fail because reason]. An AI solution could help by [specific intervention]."
TEST 1 — MEASURABILITY
Can you put a number on the problem getting better or worse? "Reduces wait time by X minutes" = measurable. "Makes things better" = not measurable.
TEST 2 — DATA AVAILABILITY
Can you actually collect data for this problem in your city? If data doesn't exist anywhere, you can't train an AI. Plan your data source before committing to the topic.
TEST 3 — AI RELEVANCE
Would AI genuinely help here? Or would a simpler solution (a notice board, a gate lock, a reminder app) work just as well? AI should be necessary, not just impressive.
ITERATIVE NATURE
You WILL revise your problem statement. When you collect data (Stage 2) you may discover the real problem is different. This is normal! Google and ISRO both do this. Iteration = progress.
// WEAK vs STRONG — CAN YOU SPOT THE DIFFERENCE?
❌ "Students in my school struggle with maths."
No number. No specific group. No location. No measurable outcome. An AI engineer cannot start designing anything from this. Fails all 3 tests.
✅ "58% of Class IX students at 3 Secunderabad municipal schools score below 40 in maths, with the gap widening in fractions and algebra topics as tracked in 2024 school records."
Specific school group ✅ Measurable (58%, below 40) ✅ Location ✅ Data source specified ✅ Topic areas named ✅ Passes all 3 tests!

Types & Methods
📦 No data = No AI. Bad data = Dangerous AI. Right data = MAGIC!
📦
// 4 TYPES OF DATA — KNOW YOUR INGREDIENTS
🏷️
Primary Data
You collect it yourself — surveys, direct observation, experiments. Fresh and specific to your exact problem. Examples: counting vehicles outside school, surveying classmates, measuring water waste. Takes time but gives you exactly what you need.
📚
Secondary Data
Already collected by someone else — government reports, research papers, hospital records, weather databases. Faster to access. Examples: IMD rainfall data, census data, CBSE school performance records. May not perfectly fit your problem but saves weeks of collection time.
📋
Structured Data
Organised in rows and columns — a spreadsheet or database table. Every record has the same fields. Examples: student marks register, patient admission records, bank transactions. AI can process this easily. About 20% of all data worldwide is structured.
📝
Unstructured Data
No fixed format — text, images, audio, video, social media posts. Harder for computers to process but contains the richest information. About 80% of all data is unstructured. ChatGPT, DALL-E, and Gemini were all trained on unstructured data!
// THE 5 QUESTIONS TO ASK BEFORE YOU COLLECT ANY DATA
  • 1

    What features (data points) do I need?

    A feature is one measurable characteristic. For a student performance AI: attendance %, homework completion rate, distance from school, parental education level, test score. Choose features that genuinely affect the outcome!

  • 2

    How will each feature affect the outcome?

    If "distance from school" is a feature — does a longer commute really affect marks? Think it through before collecting. Irrelevant features waste time and confuse the AI model.

  • 3

    Where can I reliably get this data?

    Primary sources (your own collection) OR secondary sources (government databases, school records, IMD, NITI Aayog). Always prefer official sources over random websites.

  • 4

    How often must I collect it?

    Weather AI needs hourly readings. Student performance AI needs term-wise data. Traffic AI needs minute-by-minute data. Frequency depends on how fast your problem changes.

  • 5

    What if I don't have enough data?

    An AI trained on too few examples will be wrong more often than right. Options: collect more, use data augmentation, look for a publicly available dataset, or narrow your problem scope to one that has existing data.

// WHAT IS A STAKEHOLDER?
👥 Stakeholders — Everyone Touched by Your AI
A stakeholder is anyone affected by your AI project — positively OR negatively. This includes: people who will USE the AI, people who will be ANALYSED by the AI, people who will OPERATE it, people who might be HARMED by it, and people who FUND or BUILD it. Identifying ALL stakeholders before building prevents unintended harm and ensures your solution actually helps the right people. Skipping this step is why many AI systems cause real-world damage!
// 4 TYPES OF STAKEHOLDERS WITH EXAMPLES
👤
Primary Stakeholders
People DIRECTLY affected — whose problem you are solving. Example: Diabetic patients in rural areas who benefit from AI eye-screening. Their needs drive every design decision. Always interview or survey them first!
🏥
Secondary Stakeholders
People INDIRECTLY affected. Example: Rural health workers who operate the AI tool, hospital administrators who fund it, health ministry that sets policy. They enable or constrain the solution through resources and regulations.
⚠️
At-Risk Stakeholders
People who might be HARMED — even unintentionally. Example: Patients misdiagnosed by AI, doctors whose jobs may be displaced, communities whose data was collected without clear consent. ALWAYS identify these before launching!
🌐
Society at Large
The broader community changed by widespread AI use. Example: If every school uses an AI tutor, it changes how human teachers work, how education is valued, and who has access to learning. AI systems reshape society — think big!
// AI ETHICS CHECKLIST — 5 QUESTIONS FOR YOUR PROJECT
QUESTION 1 — CONSENT
Have the people whose data you are collecting given clear permission? Do they understand how their data will be used, stored, and shared? No consent = ethical violation from Day 1.
QUESTION 2 — BIAS
Does your data represent ALL groups fairly? Could the AI perform worse for certain communities — by age, gender, income, language, or disability? Unequal data = discriminatory AI.
QUESTION 3 — PRIVACY
Are you collecting sensitive personal data — health, location, income, religion? How will you store and protect it? Who else will access it? If data leaks, who is harmed?
QUESTION 4 — ACCOUNTABILITY
If the AI makes a harmful mistake — a wrong diagnosis, an unjust rejection — WHO is responsible? The student team? The school? The AI tool provider? Accountability must be pre-defined.
QUESTION 5 — TRANSPARENCY
Can affected people understand how the AI makes its decisions? Can they challenge or appeal an AI output? A person denied a loan by AI has the right to know WHY — even in Class IX projects!
RESEARCH EXISTING SOLUTIONS
Before proposing your AI solution, check: What already exists? Why is it insufficient? Is it a technology gap, resource gap, or awareness gap? Build something GENUINELY new and useful — not a reinvention!
